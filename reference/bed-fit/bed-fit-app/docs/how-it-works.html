Bed Fit · engineering notes

Plan to Placement

How an uploaded floor plan turns into a bed against a particular wall, with a walkway measured on each side — what the pixels can tell us, what only the printed text can, and where the whole thing honestly gives up.

The job sounds like one problem and is really two. Finding where the rooms are is geometry. Finding which room is a bedroom is not geometry at all, and that distinction decides the shape of everything downstream.

Nothing in the pixels separates a 10×12 bedroom from a 10×12 kitchen. They are the same rectangle, bounded by the same walls, at the same scale. The only thing that says which is which is the word printed inside it. So the reader has to read.

That is why the pipeline splits the work between two readers that are each reliable at something the other cannot do at all: OCR for the lettering, pixel analysis for the structure. Neither is trusted with the other's job.

STAGE 01Reading the room names

Tesseract runs in --psm 11 — sparse text. That matters: the default page modes assume paragraphs, and on a plan they find one bedroom label and miss the other. Sparse mode finds both, at 79% and 96% confidence.

A word is trusted as a room name only above 45% confidence. Dimension strings are held to a much lower bar, because they OCR badly and the regex is a far stronger validator than the confidence score — a string either parses as two feet-and-inches pairs or it does not.

The bug that eats a leading digit

Tesseract split one dimension into three separate words — |, 3'4"X, 10'0" — reading the 1 of “13” as a pipe character. Parsing words individually yields 3'4": a 13-foot room reported as 3 feet, silently, with a plausible-looking number.

The fix is two-part. Words are merged into printed lines first (cluster by vertical overlap, then join left-to-right, breaking only on a wide gap). Then the usual OCR confusions are mapped — | l I → 1, O o → 0, smart quotes to straight. ||3'4"X10'0" then parses to exactly (13.333, 10.0).

STAGE 02Finding the walls

A drawn wall is a long dark run, so summing ink down each column and across each row makes walls stand out as peaks. On a real 450×700 plan this finds every wall: verticals at 44.5 / 243.5 / 393.5 and horizontals at 51.5 / 130.5 / 226.5 / 278.5 / 403.5 / 511 / 616.5 — including partial walls that span only one column of the plan.

The room rectangle is then grown outward from its label until a real wall stops it. A boundary counts as a wall only if ink actually runs along it, which is what discounts the stray lines furniture leaves behind. With text erased first, this traces a bedroom to 191×142 px against a true 192×143.

STAGE 03Fixing the scale

Everything downstream is in feet, so one number has to convert pixels to feet. Each room that printed a size votes: compare its printed dimensions to its measured rectangle and that ratio is a candidate scale.

01
Collect votesEvery labelled room with both a parsed size and a traced rectangle.
02
Drop incoherent onesIf a room's two sides imply scales more than 12% apart, its rectangle was traced badly. Discard.
03
Sanity-check the buildingA vote implying an overall plan under 8 ft or over 200 ft is nonsense. Discard.
04
Take the medianNot the first or the most confident — the middle, so one bad trace cannot carry the plan.
05
Require agreementIf votes exist but fewer than two agree within 15%, return no scale and ask. Guessing here is worse than asking.

This step exists because of a concrete failure: one room whose rectangle leaked into its neighbours voted for a scale 2.3× wrong, and being the first vote found, it set the scale for the entire plan. The median plus the plausibility check now discards it.

STAGE 04Placing the bed

With width, depth and openings known, the engine is deterministic — no model, no heuristics that vary run to run. It applies the brand guide in four moves.

Choose the headboard wall

The wall with no opening in it. Each wall is penalised by what it carries — a door is worse than a doorway is worse than a window — and the lowest score wins; among equally clear walls, the longer one, because it leaves more room on each side. When no wall is clear the engine says so rather than pretending.

Centre the bed in the free run

A door sweeps a square of its own width just inside the room. Whatever run of the headboard wall that leaves is the space the bed is centred in — and a swing that stops short of the foot of the bed does not constrain it at all.

Prefer one good side to two bad ones

If a bed cannot clear the 1′6″ minimum on both sides, symmetry is the wrong goal: two unusable gaps help nobody. The bed is pinned 6″ off the far wall so the entry side keeps one real walkway.

Grade it, then pick

RECOMMENDED when both sides clear 1′6″ and there is 2′0″ past the foot; FITS_WITH_CAVEAT when it goes in but does not; DOES_NOT_FIT when it will not go in at all. The recommendation is the largest bed that earns RECOMMENDED. Nightstands are drawn only on a side with 1′6″ to spare, which is why the king above gets none.

LIMITSWhere it gives up, on purpose

Two failures are structural rather than a matter of tuning, and both were hit on the first real plan tested.

Furniture is indistinguishable from a wall

A bed drawn inside a room is the same length, darkness and continuity as a partition. Threshold sweeps and longest-continuous-run detection each fix one case and break another: raise the bar and walls broken by doorways vanish; lower it and furniture becomes walls.

Text under a drawing is not recoverable

Where a room label overlaps furniture, the pixels for those glyphs are simply gone. Targeted re-OCR at 3×, 4× and 6× across three page-segmentation modes never parsed it — and worse, re-OCR misread a room it had already read correctly, turning 13′4″ into 3′4″.

That last result set the policy. A confident wrong answer is worse than a visible gap. Every field records where it came from, and a room the reader is unsure about arrives flagged, with its numbers ready to correct — never presented as fact.

In practice, on a plan with two bedrooms, one comes back clean and correct with no interaction at all, and the other arrives flagged with one field to fix. Correcting it clears the flag and re-runs the recommendation immediately, with no model call — the engine is pure geometry, so re-solving is free.
